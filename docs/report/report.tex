\title{Does MAMEX's Modality Gate Trust Corrupted Text?\\
A Stress Test and a Visual-Grounding Fix}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Hatice Sebla Karabunar}
\IEEEauthorblockA{YZM 5110 Multimodal Learning, Project 1 (Dr.\ Ekrem \c{C}etinkaya), November 2026\\

\begin{abstract}
MAMEX is a cold-start recommender that lets an adaptive gate weight an image expert and a text expert. We ask what the gate does when an item's text is present but misleading. On Amazon Baby, we reproduce the MAMEX baseline from the released code (Recall@20 0.0776, NDCG@20 0.0365, against 0.0771 and 0.0363 published; Recall@10 is about 0.0025 below the published value (0.0024 with ten seeds) and neither ten seeds nor the scale of the alignment loss closes that gap). We then corrupt the text of 40\% of the cold items at test time. Recall@20 falls by 14\% and the gate does not move away from text. A parameter-free correction, Visual Grounding Regularization (VGR), which scales the text weight by the image--text cosine similarity, recovers 27--38\% of the loss at no measurable clean-data cost, but about half of that gain is also obtained by two controls that ignore the image. The result is a modest fix, not a solution. A training-free repair that blends in the text of the visually nearest warm items does better (+0.0078 Recall@20, and +0.0096 together with VGR, about 89\% of the loss), and a random-neighbour control shows that the image guidance is what helps; applying it only to the items VGR's cosine flags gains +0.0094, and +0.0105 together with VGR (about 96\%), on one noise family and three seeds; H10 below repeats it under three noise types and three rates. Separately, we document that the distributed Baby features are probably not the CLIP features the paper describes (Section~\ref{sec:repro}); this is a finding about the source, not an explanation of the gap.
\end{abstract}

cold-start recommendation, multimodal recommendation, mixture of experts, robustness, reproducibility

\section{Research Question, Scope, Contributions}

\textbf{Question.} MAMEX~\cite{nguyen2025mamex} builds cold-item embeddings by letting an adaptive gate weight an image expert and a text expert. When an item's text is present but misleading (clickbait-like), does the gate keep trusting it, and can a parameter-free image--text agreement signal reduce the damage without hurting clean accuracy?

\textbf{Scope.} Amazon Baby, item cold-start (7,050 items, 1,410 cold, 19,442 training users). Text is corrupted at test time only, at the embedding level, on a share of the cold items. One model family, one fix, three training seeds. Not in scope: other datasets, real clickbait text, other cold-start models.

\textbf{Motivation.} None of the papers surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:lit} reports performance when a modality is present but misleading.

\textbf{Contributions.} (1)~a measured reproduction of the MAMEX Baby baseline on the distributed data, with an investigation of the remaining gap; (2)~a stress-test protocol for corrupted cold-item text; (3)~VGR with two controls that isolate where its effect comes from; (4)~a failure analysis of whom VGR helps and hurts; (5)~a test-driven, packaged codebase with exact commands.

Every number below is either measured in the experiments of Section~\ref{sec:design} or taken from a cited paper. What a paper reports is attributed to it; an inference is marked as our reading.

\subsection{Synthesis (our reading)}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The papers share a pipeline.} All recommendation papers use frozen, pre-extracted content features and learn only what sits on top. Content helps items with little or no history (VBPR's cold-start AUC gain; MAMEX's multimodal-over-single-modality ablation). Text tends to be the stronger modality on Amazon (MAMEX's ablation), which makes it the natural shortcut.
\item \textbf{The reported numbers cannot be compared.} VBPR calls an item cold below 5 positives, MoToRec below 10 interactions, CLCRec and MAMEX when it has none. MoToRec reports Baby R@20 0.0347 on its cold group and 0.1077 overall; MAMEX reports Baby Rec@20 0.0771 under a different protocol. MMGCN and MoToRec split interactions at random, which cannot evaluate cold items; MAMEX and CLCRec split by item.
\item \textbf{A methodological disagreement is untested.} MoToRec argues that aligning continuous embeddings creates noise and uses discrete tokens; MAMEX stays continuous and relies on a gate. Neither corrupts its inputs, so neither claim about robustness is evidence.
\item \textbf{Reproducibility is uneven.} MAMEX, MoToRec and VBPR leave key settings or code links unstated. MAMEX does not give batch size, epochs, embedding size or hardware. CLCRec releases code but reports single runs.
\item \textbf{The gap.} The only paper that studies a failing modality, DGMRec, treats it as absent, and assumes the features that are present are correct. None of the six papers tests a modality that is present but misleading (the search was targeted, so other work may exist). That is the question this project asks of MAMEX's gate, using VGR as a candidate fix.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Method: MAMEX as Implemented}

We read the model implementation of the reproduced code, then checked it against the paper's equations (last item).
\begin{itemize}
\item Frozen pre-extracted image and text features, each L2-normalised, pass through a separate mixture-of-experts layer (4 linear experts, top-2 chosen by a learned gate, softmax over the two selected logits), giving a 128-d embedding per modality and a load-balancing loss.
\item The two embeddings are concatenated and passed to a fusion gate (an MLP with ReLU and dropout) with two outputs; a softmax gives $[w_v, w_t]$. The item embedding is $w_v e_{\text{img}} + w_t e_{\text{txt}}$.
\item User embeddings are free parameters. The loss is BPR, plus L2 regularisation, an alignment term (MSE between the item embedding and each modality embedding), the expert balance loss, and a KL term keeping the mean fusion gate near uniform. Coefficients are the repository defaults (align 1, reg $10^{-6}$, moe\_balance 1, fusion\_balance 1).
\item Cold items are scored by the inner product of the user embedding with an item embedding built only from their modality features.
\item \emph{Against the paper} (arXiv 2508.08042v1, Eqs.~3--11). The MoE adapter (Eq.~3), BPR loss (Eq.~10), alignment loss (Eq.~8), KL balance term (Eq.~7) and loss sum (Eq.~11) match the code in structure. Two differences in detail: (1)~Eq.~5 describes a \emph{sparse} softmax gate over the concatenated embeddings, while the code uses a small MLP and an ordinary softmax over two outputs; the two agree unless the paper's sparsity keeps only one modality, which the paper does not say. (2)~The text after Eq.~8 calls the alignment loss an MSE, but the equation is a squared L2 norm; the code uses the mean squared error, smaller by a factor equal to the embedding size (128). The paper's weight $\lambda_1 \in \{1, 0.1, 0.01\}$ does not say which scale it refers to. The expert-balance term (Eq.~4) was not checked line by line.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproduction Target, Implementation, Deviations}
\label{sec:repro}

\textbf{Target.} Published, Amazon Baby, kept apart from measured numbers (Table~1 of the paper): Rec@10 0.0512, Rec@20 0.0771, NDCG@10 0.0292, NDCG@20 0.0363.

\textbf{Implementation.} The upstream repository (commit \code{88a932e}) was restructured into a package, with characterisation tests added before the restructure and unit and end-to-end tests after (149 tests). Training and evaluation logic are unchanged. Added: test-time corruption, VGR with controls, $\gamma$ selection on validation data, the evaluation report, saved per-pair ranks and, for the ablation in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}, an optional training-time corruption of warm items (\code{--train\_noise\_rate}, default off).

\subsection{Deviation 1: features (paper says CLIP, files probably are not)}

\textbf{What the paper and the authors say.} The MAMEX paper describes CLIP features, and the repository README states ``CLIP-based feature extraction, inspired by MMSRec'' (as quoted in issue \#2 below). The authors' repository hosts only the processed Baby data. An open issue on that repository (\#2, August 2025) asks for the preprocessing steps and the raw-data source. A collaborator's reply (19 September 2025) points to the feature-extraction code in MMSRec~\cite{mmsrec} and to McAuley's raw Amazon data. The thread names no extraction script. MMSRec's README (read 2026-10-23) names CLIP as the extractor but does not give the variant or the feature dimensions, and lists Beauty, Sports, Clothing, Home and MovieLens-1M as recommendation datasets, not Baby. The pointer therefore does not by itself give a way to regenerate the Baby features.

\textbf{What the distributed files contain} (measured on \code{image_feat.npy} and \code{text_feat.npy}, 7,050 items):
\begin{itemize}
\item Image: 4,096-d, all values $\ge 0$, 79.2\% exactly zero, row norms 36--153.
\item Text: 1,024-d, dense and signed (values $-12.9$ to $8.1$, no zeros), row norms 23--30.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Why this points away from CLIP} (our reading).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Dimension.} CLIP's released image encoders output embeddings of 512 to 1,024 dimensions~\cite{radford2021clip}; none outputs 4,096. (The last ResNet stage of RN50x64 is 4,096 channels wide (Table~19 of the paper), but CLIP does not output it as the embedding, and a spatially pooled map of it would not have 79\% exact zeros; this is our reading.) The 1,024-d text dimension alone does not discriminate: both CLIP text encoders and sentence-transformers models can output it.
\item \emph{Shape of the values.} Non-negative, 79\% sparse vectors of 4,096 dimensions are what a ReLU-gated fully connected layer of an ImageNet CNN produces (VBPR's 4096-d fc7 features~\cite{he2016vbpr} have this form); CLIP's output embeddings are dense and signed.
\item \emph{Independent description of Baby.} LATTICE~\cite{zhang2021lattice} uses the same dataset (19,445 users, 7,050 items) with ``the 4,096-dimensional visual features that have been extracted and published'' and 1,024-d sentence-transformers text embeddings. DGMRec~\cite{kim2025dgmrec} states that its Amazon features come from ``a CNN model and SBERT''. The dimensions match the distributed files.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What is not shown.} We cannot prove it. The item ids in the files are not mapped to Amazon ASINs, so the vectors cannot be matched to the published 2014 features, and no CLIP features were extracted for comparison (that would need the raw images and titles and the id-to-ASIN mapping). The three pieces of evidence agree, but the conclusion remains an inference.

\textbf{What follows for this project.} (i)~Everything below runs on the distributed features; the reproduction target is therefore ``the released code on the released data'', not ``MAMEX on CLIP features''. (ii)~This is a finding about how well the source is documented, not an explanation of our results. We do not offer it as the cause of the Rec@10 gap: Rec@20 and NDCG@20 match the published values, and no experiment here varied the features. (iii)~The stress test and VGR are defined on embeddings and make no assumption about the extractor, but the size of the loss and of VGR's recovery may differ with other features. This is listed under limitations.

\subsection{Other deviations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Unstated settings.} Batch size, epochs, embedding size and hardware are not given in the paper; repository defaults are used (batch 2,048; 128-d; up to 1,000 epochs; early stop 200).
\item \emph{Selection.} The best epoch is chosen on clean validation NDCG@10.
\item \emph{Seeds.} Three seeds (2025--2027) for all main results; the paper's seed protocol is not stated.
\item \emph{Reproduction criterion.} The claim tested is that the released code, trained on the distributed data, gives the published Baby numbers. It is questioned for a metric if the published value lies outside the range of the seed results. This criterion was written after the baseline had been seen, so it is not pre-registered. Table~\ref{tab:repro} applies it: three of four metrics pass and Rec@10 misses by 0.0005, so the reproduction is close but not exact. The ten-seed check in Section~\ref{sec:results} is stricter: there NDCG@10 also falls outside two standard errors of the published value. The diagnostic checks did not close the gap.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proposed Improvement and Rationale}

\textbf{Visual Grounding Regularization (VGR).} After the gate,
\begin{equation}
w_t' = w_t \cdot \sigma\bigl(\gamma \cos(e_{\text{img}}, e_{\text{txt}})\bigr),
\end{equation}
then $[w_v, w_t']$ is renormalised to sum to 1. The cosine uses the 128-d post-MoE embeddings and carries no gradient. There are no learned parameters, so VGR can be switched on or off on the same clean-trained checkpoint (the default; training with VGR is an ablation only).

\textbf{Rationale} (stated before results). The alignment loss pulls the two embeddings together on clean data, so a low cosine signals that the text disagrees with the visual evidence; the sigmoid turns that into a lower text weight. A cost known in advance: $\sigma(\cdot) < 1$ always, so text is also down-weighted on clean items. This is measured, not assumed away.

\textbf{Hypotheses and what would falsify them} (written on 16 October 2026, before the first full-training results of 17 October; the repository history was built on 27 October, after the experiments, so this date is our statement, not a commit timestamp).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{H1, the failure exists.} With 40\% of cold items' text corrupted, Recall@20 and NDCG@20 fall and the gate does not shift away from text (mean $w_t$ on corrupted items stays within $\pm0.05$ of its clean value). We would doubt it if the drop is within one seed std of zero, or if $w_t$ falls by more than 0.10 (the gate already adapts). The gate sees only the post-MoE embeddings and was trained on clean data, where text and image agree, so nothing taught it that text can be confidently wrong.
\item \textbf{H2, VGR reduces it.} On the same clean-trained checkpoint, VGR lowers $w_t$ on corrupted items and raises noisy Recall@20 and NDCG@20; expected order: clean baseline $>$ VGR (noisy) $>$ stress test. No magnitude is predicted. We would doubt it if there is no improvement or if the sign is not the same in all three seeds.
\item \textbf{H3, the cost is small.} On clean input VGR changes Recall@20 by less than one seed std and the forward pass slows by less than 5\%. We would doubt it if the clean drop exceeds one seed std, which would mean robustness is bought with clean quality.
\item \textbf{H4, the gain comes from grounding, not from muting text.} Two controls, shuffled cosine (each item gets another item's cosine) and constant factor (every item gets the mean VGR factor), recover less than VGR on noisy input. If either matches VGR, the benefit is only ``trust text less'' and the grounding explanation is not supported.
\item \textbf{H5, noise-augmented training (added 28 October, after H1--H4 and their results were known, before any augmented run).} Training with 40\% of the warm items' text corrupted in every epoch (new mask and prototypes each epoch; cold items are never seen in training) raises the noisy Recall@20 above the stress-test model's in all three seeds (H5a), costs clean Recall@20 less than the noise costs the baseline (H5b), and leaves VGR a smaller gain on top (H5c). The training noise is the same family as the test noise, the best case for augmentation.
\item \textbf{H6, where the gate of the augmented model collapses (added 28 October, after the H5 results, before the probe runs).} On warm items, the text weight of the augmented model is lower on corrupted items than on uncorrupted ones by more than 0.2 (H6a), the uncorrupted ones stay above 0.8 as the fusion balance loss would require (H6b), and the clean-trained control shows no such gap (H6c). Doubt conditions: a gap below 0.1, or a text weight above 0.9 everywhere.
\item \textbf{H7, where the cold items sit (added 29 October, after the H6 results, before the probe runs).} In the space the gate reads (the concatenated image and text expert outputs), the cold items lie at least 1.2 times as far from the warm centroid as warm items do in the augmented model (H7a), the augmented ratio is within 20\% of the control's (H7b), and across cold items the distance correlates with $w_t$ at Spearman above 0.3 (H7c). Doubt conditions: a ratio below 1.1, or a correlation below 0.1.
\item \textbf{H8, training-free repair of suspect text (added 29 October, after H5--H7 and after VGR's small gain were known, before any repair run).} On the three saved clean-trained checkpoints, two repairs replace part of a cold item's text by the mean text of its ten nearest warm items in image space: a blind blend of every item (M1) and a gated blend (M2) whose weight rises when the item's text disagrees with that neighbour text. M2 beats the unrepaired model and VGR in all three seeds (H8a); both repairs beat random-neighbour controls and M2 beats a permuted-score control (H8b); M1 costs clean text more than 0.003 and M2 less than 0.002 Recall@20 (H8c); the agreement score separates corrupted from clean items with AUROC at least 0.7 (H8d); building the repair takes under 1~s and 100~MB and leaves the forward time within 5\% (H8e). Parameters are chosen on noisy validation data only.
\item \textbf{H9, repair only the flagged items (added 29 October, after the H8 results, before the runs).} Because VGR's cosine finds the corrupted items almost perfectly, a repair applied only to the items it flags (M3) gains more than the blind blend M1 (H9a), beats random-neighbour and permuted-cosine controls in all three seeds (H9b), changes clean text by less than 0.002 Recall@20 (H9c) and costs under 0.1~s (H9d). An oracle that repairs exactly the corrupted items was added as an upper bound.
\item \textbf{H10, other noise and mismatched validation (added 29 October, after the H9 results, before the runs).} On the same checkpoints, M3 beats the unrepaired model in all three seeds in at least eight of nine settings (hashtag, shuffle, Gaussian at rates 0.2, 0.4, 0.6) and VGR on average (H10a), beats both controls in every setting (H10b), is at least as good as M1 in at least six (H10c), gains more at rate 0.6 than at 0.2 (H10d), changes clean text by less than 0.003 (H10e), and, with validation noise fixed at hashtag 0.4, keeps at least 70\% of its gain in four mismatch settings (H10f).
\item \textbf{H11, five seeds for the main setting (added 29 October, after the H10 results, before the runs).} Two more seeds (2028, 2029), trained in PyTorch 2.9.1: M3 beats the unrepaired model and its controls in both (H11a, H11b), and is at least as good as M1 in at least four of five seeds, with the bootstrap interval of the difference reported without a prediction (H11c).
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Data and split.} Amazon Baby item cold-start split as distributed: cold items never appear in training; validation and test contain cold items only (705 items each, disjoint). Evaluation candidates are all 1,410 cold items.
\item \textbf{Noise protocol} (design decision). Test time only, on cold items' text features; training and validation stay clean. This is the standard robustness protocol, the clean control is exact, and training on noisy text would let the gate learn to hide the weakness. The same seed gives the same corruption mask in every mode. \emph{Hashtag} noise (main): a corrupted item's unit text vector is mixed (weight 0.8) with one of 5 shared ``clickbait'' prototype vectors drawn from warm items, imitating text dominated by generic trending tags. Also implemented: \emph{shuffle} and \emph{gaussian}. Rate 0.4 (564 of 1,410 cold items). The noise is simulated on embeddings because only pre-extracted features exist; it is not real clickbait text.
\item \textbf{Conditions.} Baseline (clean); stress test (noisy, no VGR); MAMEX-VGR (noisy, $\gamma$ chosen on noisy validation data from $\{1,2,4,8,16\}$ with a different mask, ties to the smaller $\gamma$); clean text with VGR; shuffled-cosine and constant-factor controls.
\item \textbf{Metrics.} Recall, NDCG and the repository's HR at $K \in \{10,\dots,50\}$ (HR divides hits by $\min(K, \text{positives})$); headline Recall@20 and NDCG@20. Mean $\pm$ sample std over three seeds and per-seed paired differences. In addition, from the saved per-pair ranks: Success@$K$ (share of users with at least one test item in the top $K$; this is the usual hit rate, not the repository's HR) and MRR (mean of 1/best rank per user), each with 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals over test users (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty}). No significance test is claimed with $n=3$.
\item \textbf{No tuning on the test set.} $\gamma$ and the best epoch use validation data only; a test-set $\gamma$ sweep is a sensitivity table reported after selection.
\item \textbf{Cost.} Forward latency with and without VGR, GPU memory and training seconds, from the same runs.
\item \textbf{Resources.} One RTX A4000 and an Intel i9-12900K; about 18--20 minutes of training per run when run alone. Environment, data checksums, commands and cost are in Appendix~\ref{app:record}.
\item \textbf{Ablation (H5).} Noise-augmented training as above, three seeds, evaluated like the core grid ($\gamma$ selected on noisy validation data, best epoch on clean validation); results in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}. Not done: other noise types in training, other augmentation rates or designs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results, Controls, Uncertainty, Failure Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

All numbers are computed from the saved per-run outputs (metrics, gate statistics, latency and per-pair ranks). Test split, cold items only, hashtag noise at rate 0.4, mean $\pm$ std over seeds 2025--2027. A repeated run of the whole core grid reproduced every saved number exactly (timing and memory excluded) when it was made, between 17 and 23 October. On 28 October this no longer held: with the installed PyTorch 2.14.1 (CUDA 13.0), not the pinned 2.9.1, a clean baseline of seed 2026 gives Recall@20 0.0753 (best epoch 480) instead of the saved 0.0736 (410), with the same code. The code is not the cause (the pre-ablation code gives the same 0.0753); runs made on 28 October repeat each other exactly, alone and six at a time. On 29 October we reinstalled the pinned PyTorch 2.9.1 (CUDA 12.8) in a separate environment and retrained the same model: it reproduces the saved result exactly (best epoch 410, Recall@20 0.07361 and NDCG@20 0.03410, and the noisy block equal as well), so for this seed the PyTorch version, not the code, explains the difference. The other two seeds, retrained there with checkpoints, reproduce their saved baselines exactly as well (epochs 480 and 470, Recall@20 0.08193 and 0.07741, clean and noisy blocks equal). The core, sweep and diagnostic results are as saved from the earlier runs; the ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) is compared with a clean-trained control made in the same environment.

\textbf{D1, seed variation.} Rule: if the published value lies within two standard errors of the ten-seed mean, seed variation is consistent with the gap; if not, it is not enough on its own. We also report the share of seeds at or above the published value. Seven more baseline seeds (2028--2034) give ten in total. Mean $\pm$ standard error: Rec@10 $0.0488 \pm 0.0004$, Rec@20 $0.0773 \pm 0.0009$, NDCG@10 $0.0279 \pm 0.0004$, NDCG@20 $0.0358 \pm 0.0005$. The published Rec@20 and NDCG@20 lie within two standard errors of the mean; the published Rec@10 (0.0512) and NDCG@10 (0.0292) do not. None of the ten seeds reaches the published Rec@10 (best 0.0507); for NDCG@10 three of ten are at or above the published value. By the stated rule, seed variation alone does not explain the Rec@10 gap, which is about 0.0024.

\textbf{D2, scale of the alignment loss.} Rule: if one weight brings mean Rec@10 within 0.001 of the published value without lowering Rec@20 or NDCG, the scale is a candidate explanation; no weight is adopted as the main baseline, because that would need a validation-based choice and a re-run of every condition. The code's MSE is 128 times smaller than the paper's squared norm, so the paper's weights $\{0.01, 0.1, 1\}$ correspond to \code{--align_coeff} $\{1.28, 12.8, 128\}$.

\subsection{Hypotheses}

\textbf{H1, the failure exists: supported.} Corrupting 40\% of cold items' text lowers Recall@20 from 0.0776 to 0.0668 ($-0.0109$, 14\% relative) and NDCG@20 from 0.0365 to 0.0307. The gate does not move away from text on the corrupted items: $w_t$ rises from 0.550 to 0.563, a small change in the wrong direction.

\textbf{H2, VGR reduces it: partly supported, the effect is small.} Noisy VGR beats noisy in 3/3 seeds, by $+0.0033$ Recall@20 and $+0.0012$ NDCG@20. That recovers 27--38\% of the Recall@20 loss (28, 27, 38\% by seed) and 20--23\% of the NDCG@20 loss; most of the loss remains. The gate moves toward the image ($w_t$ 0.563 $\to$ 0.416).

\textbf{H3, small clean cost: supported for quality, not for latency.} Clean VGR $-$ clean is $-0.0004$ Recall@20 and $-0.0002$ NDCG@20, positive in 1 of 3 seeds and smaller than the seed std. The pre-stated latency limit (slowdown below 5\%) is missed: the forward pass rises by 0.36~ms, or 5.6\% (one GPU, per-seed timings 6.4--7.0~ms). Memory is unchanged.

\textbf{H4, the gain comes from grounding: partly supported.} At the selected $\gamma = 1$ the two controls recover about half of VGR's gain (shuffled $+0.0017$, constant $+0.0015$, against $+0.0033$). VGR beats both in 3/3 seeds, so part of the effect depends on the cosine, but about half comes from muting text without looking at the image. The grounding-specific part is about $+0.0017$ Recall@20, roughly 15\% of the loss.

\textbf{H5, noise-augmented training: not supported (H5a), supported (H5b), supported in size but not in mechanism (H5c).} See Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.

\textbf{H6, gate probe: not supported (H6a, H6b), supported (H6c).} See Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.

\textbf{H7, representation probe: not supported (H7a, H7c), supported but empty (H7b).} See Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.

\textbf{H8, training-free repair: supported (H8a, H8e), not supported (H8b, H8c, H8d).} See Section~\ref{sec:repair}.

\textbf{H9, repair only the flagged items: supported (H9a--H9d); the oracle is not an upper bound.} See Section~\ref{sec:repair}.

\textbf{H10, other noise and mismatched validation: supported (H10a, H10b, H10d, H10e, H10f), not supported (H10c).} See Section~\ref{sec:repair}.

\subsection{Ablation: noise-augmented training}
\label{sec:ablation}

The model was trained with 40\% of the warm items' hashtag-noise text corrupted in every epoch (Section~\ref{sec:design}, H5) and evaluated like the core runs. Means over seeds 2025--2027 (Recall@20 / NDCG@20): clean text 0.0725 / 0.0325; noisy text 0.0568 / 0.0261; noisy text with VGR 0.0571 / 0.0263. The clean-trained model scores 0.0776 / 0.0365 on clean text and 0.0668 / 0.0307 on noisy text.

\emph{H5a, not supported.} The augmented model is worse than the stress-test model on noisy text in 3/3 seeds ($-0.0106$, $-0.0082$, $-0.0111$ Recall@20; mean $-0.0100$), and the loss left against the clean baseline grows from 0.0108 to 0.0209. \emph{H5b, supported.} Its clean Recall@20 is 0.0052 below the clean baseline (3/3 seeds), less than the 0.0108 that noise costs the baseline, but both conditions end up lower than with clean training. \emph{H5c, supported in size.} VGR adds $+0.0003$ Recall@20 on the augmented model (per seed $+0.0002$, $+0.0006$, $0.0000$) against $+0.0033$ on the clean-trained one. The stated reason, that the gate has already learned to discount disagreeing text, is not what the gate shows: on the corrupted cold items $w_t$ is 0.917 (seed std 0.048) with noisy text and 0.986 with clean text, against 0.563 and 0.550 in the clean-trained model. The gate of the augmented models puts almost all weight on text.

\emph{Gate probe (H6).} To see where the gate collapses we retrained six models (three seeds, with and without augmentation, in the same environment) and measured $w_t$ on warm items, which the models were trained on. In the augmented model $w_t$ is 0.313 (std 0.010) on warm items corrupted by a fresh training-style mask, 0.435 on the others and 0.436 with no noise, but 0.987 on clean cold items. The clean-trained control gives 0.495, 0.487, 0.487 and 0.549. H6a is not supported: the gap between uncorrupted and corrupted warm items is 0.12, 0.11 and 0.13, in the predicted direction but below the 0.2 set in advance (the control gap is $-0.029$, $+0.008$, $-0.005$, so H6c holds). H6b is not supported: the uncorrupted warm items sit at 0.435, not above 0.8, so the explanation that the balance loss forces the gate to compensate on warm items is not what the numbers show. What the numbers do show is that the collapse belongs to cold items: after augmentation the gate gives the warm items about 0.44 and the unseen cold items 0.99, while the clean-trained model gives 0.49 and 0.55. In the same environment the augmented model is below its clean-trained control in all three seeds, by $-0.0105$ Recall@20 on noisy text and $-0.0054$ on clean text, close to the differences against the saved core runs ($-0.0100$ and $-0.0052$). We still do not know why augmentation changes the gate on cold items only; the candidate that the cold items' expert outputs lie far from the warm items was tested next (H7, below). The runs show that this particular augmentation (rate 0.4, prototype weight 0.8, new prototypes every epoch, hashtag noise) did not make the model more robust and shifted the gate towards text on unseen items. A fixed set of prototypes, a lower rate, noise on the image side or a different balance loss were not tried, and with three seeds and one design we cannot say that noise-augmented training does not work in general, only that it did not here. Training took 2,333--2,554~s per run with three running together on one GPU, so the time is not comparable to the solo runs. The per-seed numbers are in the repository's tables output.

\emph{Representation probe (H7).} We retrained the same six models and measured, on clean text, where the cold items sit in the 256-dimensional space the gate reads. In the augmented model the distance of a cold item to the warm centroid is 1.72 on average against 2.37 for warm items, a ratio of 0.720, 0.719 and 0.734 over the three seeds, so the cold items are closer to the warm centroid, not farther (H7a not supported; the doubt condition was a ratio below 1.1). The control's ratio is 0.784, 0.788 and 0.778, within 20\% of the augmented one, so H7b holds, but with H7a failing it says only that the two models place cold items alike by this measure. Across cold items, the distance correlates with $w_t$ at Spearman $+0.08$, $+0.15$ and $+0.14$ (H7c needed above 0.3, not supported). What differs is the gate's answer: the mean logit difference (text minus image) on cold items is $+11.7$ in the augmented model against $-0.14$ on warm items, and $+0.20$ against $-0.05$ in the control. The expert outputs of cold items are also smaller in both models, in the augmented one clearly (image norm 1.16 against 1.93 on warm items, text 1.25 against 1.50; control 2.09 against 2.78 and 2.31 against 2.71). This was not predicted and we did not test whether the shrunken norm drives the gate; it is an observation, not an explanation. So the distance to the warm centroid does not explain the collapse, and the cause is still unknown. The six runs equal the earlier gate-probe runs bit for bit.

\subsection{Training-free text repair}
\label{sec:repair}
VGR lowers the weight of a suspect text but puts nothing in its place, and its gain is small. We therefore tested two repairs on the three saved clean-trained checkpoints (retrained once in the pinned PyTorch 2.9.1 environment, equal to the core baselines; the model, its weights and its forward pass are unchanged). Both build a replacement for a cold item's text from the mean unit text of its ten nearest warm items by image cosine, $t_{\mathrm{knn}}$. M1 blends it into every cold item, $t' = (1-\lambda)\,t + \lambda\, t_{\mathrm{knn}}$; M2 sets the weight per item from the agreement $a_i = \cos(t_i, t_{\mathrm{knn},i})$ as $\lambda_{\max}\,\sigma(-g\,(a_i - \mathrm{median}(a)))$. $\lambda$, $\lambda_{\max}$ and $g$ are chosen on noisy validation data (NDCG@10, mask drawn from seed$+10000$), never on the test set. Controls: the same repairs with ten random warm items instead of the image neighbours, and M2 with the agreement scores handed to the wrong items. The unrepaired and VGR numbers in this evaluation equal the saved runs exactly (same $\gamma = 1$).

H8a holds: M2 beats the unrepaired model in all three seeds (+0.0077, +0.0010, +0.0068) and VGR on average. H8b does not hold as stated: both repairs beat their random-neighbour controls in all three seeds (M1 by +0.0080, M2 by +0.0047), so the image guidance is what helps, but M2 loses to its permuted-agreement control in all three seeds ($-0.0020$), so its per-item score adds nothing. This fits the detection numbers (H8d, not supported): the AUROC of the agreement score for finding the 564 corrupted cold items is 0.511, 0.559 and 0.708, against 0.999 in every seed for VGR's cosine of the two expert outputs. The simple blind blend M1, which was not a headline hypothesis, is the best single method: +0.0078 in all three seeds, with $\lambda$ 0.75, 0.75 and 0.5 chosen on validation, and followed by VGR it reaches 0.0764, +0.0096 over the unrepaired model, about 89\% of the 0.0109 lost to the corruption (VGR alone: about 31\%). H8c does not hold: M1 costs clean text only $-0.0007$ Recall@20 (M2 +0.0008), which also says that the nearest warm items' text is almost as informative as the item's own text for these features. H8e holds: the neighbour search takes 0.60~s once, building the repaired features 0.015~s, the extra memory is 41~MB (computed from the array sizes) and the forward pass 7.88~ms against 8.07~ms with repaired text (+2.4\%).

We read this with care. The corruption is the one used throughout, a mix of the item's text with one of five warm-item vectors; blending in neighbour text dilutes that mix, so the gain of a blind blend may be specific to it; H10 below tests other noise. There are three seeds and one dataset. The M2 random-neighbour control was left out of the first run by oversight and added afterwards on the same checkpoints; no other number changed. One unpredicted observation: VGR's cosine detects the corrupted items almost perfectly, so detection is not what limits VGR; what it does with a detected item may be. H9 tests this next.

\emph{Repairing only the flagged items (H9, written after the H8 results, before the runs).} M3 blends in the neighbour text only for items whose image--text cosine (the quantity VGR uses, one extra forward pass) lies below a quantile $q$ of the cosines over the cold items: $\lambda_i = \lambda_{\max}\,\sigma(g\,(Q_q - c_i))$, with $\lambda_{\max}$, $q$ and $g$ chosen on noisy validation data. We chose the cosine after seeing its AUROC in H8, and, like VGR's $\gamma$, $q$ is chosen on validation data that carry the corruption at the stated rate. M3 gains $+0.0094$ Recall@20 over the unrepaired model (0.0761, 3/3 seeds), more than the blind blend M1 in all three seeds but by little ($+0.0029$, $+0.0005$, $+0.0011$); followed by VGR it reaches 0.0772 ($+0.0105$), about 96\% of the 0.0109 lost (M3 alone about 86\%). H9a to H9d hold: M3 beats the random-neighbour control ($+0.0156$, $+0.0087$, $+0.0108$; that control loses accuracy, $-0.0023$ on noisy and $-0.0056$ on clean text) and the permuted-cosine control ($+0.0049$, $+0.0021$, $+0.0043$), it changes clean text by $+0.0016$, and the cosine costs 0.017~s and 0.005~MB. The selected $q$ is 0.5 in all seeds, not the true rate 0.4. We had added an oracle that blends only the truly corrupted items as an upper bound; it is not one: it reaches $+0.0068$ and M3 beats it in all three seeds ($+0.0022$, $+0.0036$, $+0.0020$), as does M1. So repairing exactly the corrupted items is not the best use of the neighbour text; part of the benefit comes from neighbour text on clean items too, which we did not separate further. The caveats of the previous paragraph apply, and the margin of M3 over M1 is within the seed spread.

\emph{Other noise and mismatched validation (H10, written after the H9 results, before the runs).} The same three checkpoints were evaluated under hashtag, shuffle and Gaussian noise at rates 0.2, 0.4 and 0.6, with validation noise equal to the test noise, and under four settings whose validation noise was fixed at hashtag 0.4 (Table~\ref{tab:grid}). H10a holds: M3 beats the unrepaired model in all three seeds in all nine settings and VGR on average. H10b holds on the means, but the doubt condition of a control within 0.001 is met once, for Gaussian noise at 0.4, where M3 is only $+0.0004$ above the permuted-cosine control. H10c does not hold: M3 is at least as good as the blind blend M1 in only five of nine settings, and M1 is better at rate 0.6 in every noise type, so with much corrupted text flagging items adds nothing over repairing all of them. H10d holds (more noise, larger gain in all three types), H10e holds (M3 changes clean text by $+0.0009$ to $+0.0019$), and H10f holds: with the validation noise fixed at hashtag 0.4, M3 keeps 99\% to 116\% of its matched gain in all four mismatch settings and still beats the unrepaired model in every seed, so the repair does not depend on knowing the noise rate or type that the validation data carry. The cosine detects the corrupted items with AUROC 0.993 or higher in every setting. M3 followed by VGR recovers 71\% to 124\% of the loss (99\% to 124\% at rate 0.2, 71\% to 83\% at rate 0.6); above 100\% the repaired model on noisy text is above the unrepaired model on clean text, so the neighbour text helps beyond undoing the corruption. The limits stay: one dataset, three seeds (five for the main setting), three synthetic noise types (one more than the original study), and a pattern that favours the blind blend at high noise.

\section{Limitations, Conclusions, Unresolved Questions}

\textbf{Limitations.} The reproduction is close but not exact: Rec@10 is about 0.0025 below the published value and the gap is unexplained. The distributed features are probably not the CLIP features the paper describes (Section~\ref{sec:repro}); the size of the loss and of the recovery may differ with other features. The noise is simulated on embeddings by mixing in one of five warm-item prototypes, not generated from real clickbait text. There is one dataset and three seeds; VGR was studied under one noise family at the headline rate, and the repairs of Section~\ref{sec:repair} under three simulated noise types at three rates, never under real corrupted text; paired differences agree in sign, but are of the order of the seed std (about 0.002) and no significance test is claimed. Sweeps use one seed. The selected $\gamma$ is the grid minimum for all seeds and rates; the post-hoc extension to $\gamma<1$ raises the total gain but shrinks the cosine-specific part, and the validation criterion cannot tell the two apart. Latency is noisy at this scale (6.4--7.0~ms). The environment changed between the core runs and the ablation (PyTorch 2.14.1 instead of the pinned 2.9.1), and a rerun of a core baseline no longer repeats the saved number exactly (Section~\ref{sec:results}); installing 2.9.1 again in a separate environment reproduced the saved baselines of all three seeds exactly, so the version explains it. Hyperparameters are repository defaults and were not tuned. The noise-augmented ablation is one design (rate 0.4, same noise family as the test noise, one prototype scheme) and did not help; its failure is unexplained (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). Not run: other noise types, datasets and cold-start models.

\textbf{Conclusions.} On the distributed data the released code reproduces the published Rec@20 and NDCG@20 within seed noise and falls about 0.0025 short on Rec@10, which we could not explain. MAMEX loses 14\% of its cold-item Recall@20 when 40\% of the cold items' text is corrupted, and its gate keeps trusting the text. VGR recovers about a third of that loss in all three seeds, at no measurable clean-data accuracy cost and about 0.4~ms (5.6\%) of latency, slightly above the 5\% limit set in advance. About half of the gain is also obtained by a control that does not use the image, so the grounding-specific effect is small, about 0.0017 Recall@20 at the selected $\gamma$. The share depends strongly on $\gamma$: smaller below 1, larger around 2. The gain concentrates on users who see corrupted items, and VGR slightly hurts the others. As a fix the result is modest, not a solution. Blending in the text of the visually nearest warm items, chosen on validation data, recovers about 72\% of the loss alone and about 89\% together with VGR, costs 0.6~s once and no measurable clean-text accuracy, and a random-neighbour control gains nothing; this was tested on one synthetic noise family and three seeds. Under shuffled and Gaussian noise and at rates of 0.2 to 0.6 it still beats the unrepaired model in every seed, and with a validation set whose noise differs from the test noise it keeps 99\% to 116\% of its gain, but the blind blend is as good or better at rate 0.6. Applying it only to the items VGR's cosine flags recovers about 86\% alone and about 96\% with VGR on the headline noise; the margin over the blind blend is small ($+0.0016$ on average, positive in all three seeds), and an oracle that repairs exactly the corrupted items does worse (about 62\%), so part of the benefit comes from the neighbour text on clean items too.

\textbf{Unresolved questions.} Why does training on corrupted warm items move the gate to text on cold items ($w_t$ 0.99, against 0.44 on warm items) and lower accuracy under noise (distance to the warm centroid does not explain it, H7; whether the smaller expert outputs of cold items do was not tested), and does another augmentation design behave differently? Is there a $\gamma$ selection rule that favours values whose gain depends on the cosine (for example one that also reports the shuffled-cosine control on validation data), and does the $\gamma\approx2$ pattern hold on other seeds? Does the pattern hold with real clickbait text, with genuine CLIP features, on other datasets and with training-time noise? What closes the remaining Rec@10 gap of about 0.0025, given that neither more seeds nor the alignment-loss scale does? The features, the split and unstated settings are candidates that were not tested.

\textbf{Process and turning points.} 16 October: literature search, hypotheses H1--H4 and the stress-test protocol written down, then the noise simulator, VGR and its controls implemented. 17 October: first three-seed results (baseline, stress test, VGR) and the failure analysis. The Rec@10 gap to the published value showed up in these results; on 22 October we wrote down two candidate explanations (seed variation D1, loss scale D2) before running the 16 diagnostic runs, which finished on 23 October. Neither explained the gap. Between 22 and 24 October the report was checked against the papers and the saved outputs, which led to the corrections below. On 28 October the noise-augmented ablation, left out until then, was implemented, its hypothesis H5 written down before the runs, and the three runs added. On the same day a gate probe (H6) showed that the collapse is specific to cold items, and a rerun of a core baseline showed that the saved core results are no longer reproduced bit for bit in the current environment.

\textbf{Errors found and corrected along the way.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{VGR was applied in training.} The first version of the \code{improved} mode also trained with VGR, so the VGR result was not measured on the same clean-trained model as the baseline. On 16 October, before any reported result, VGR was made an evaluation-time switch (training with VGR remains as the \code{--train_with_vgr} ablation, default off).
\item \emph{The feature assumption.} We started from the paper's statement that the features are CLIP. The shapes and value ranges of the distributed files did not fit (Section~\ref{sec:repro}), so the CLIP claim became a recorded deviation instead of an assumption.
\item \emph{Loss scale.} The code's alignment loss is a mean squared error, smaller by a factor of 128 than the paper's squared L2 norm. We first treated this as a possible cause of the Rec@10 gap; the extra runs (D2) showed it is not.
\item \emph{Literature rows.} After reading MMGCN in full on 22 October, its baselines, recall gain and mechanism were corrected in the matrix. A note on CLCRec that rested on a summary, not on the paper, was removed. The claim that no paper tests a misleading modality was narrowed to the six papers read, since the search was targeted.
\item \emph{The H3 verdict.} H3 was first written as supported although the measured slowdown (5.6\%) exceeded the 5\% limit set in advance. A later check against the stated limit found this; the verdict now reads supported for quality, not for latency. The same check aligned the Recall@10 gap (0.0025 with three seeds) across the report.
\item \emph{Reproducibility claim.} The report said that a repeated run reproduced every saved number exactly. That was true when it was checked (17--23 October), but on 28 October a rerun of a core baseline gave a different result. The installed PyTorch was 2.14.1+cu130, not the pinned 2.9.1; the earlier code reproduces the new number, so we think the environment changed. On 29 October we installed 2.9.1 in a separate environment and the same seed (2026) reproduced the saved baseline exactly, which confirms the cause; seeds 2025 and 2027 reproduce as well. The claim now says when it held and in which environment.
\item \emph{Wrong upper bound.} We labelled the oracle that repairs only the truly corrupted items an upper bound; M3 and even the blind blend beat it, so it is only a reference point and is called that now.
\item \emph{Missing control.} The H8 text named a random-neighbour control for both repairs but the first run only had it for the blind blend; it was added on the same checkpoints and no other number changed.
\item \emph{Wasted training time.} No checkpoint was saved, so every condition for a seed retrained the same clean model; one checkpoint per seed would have cut most of the 32.7 hours (Appendix~\ref{app:record}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproduction Record}
\label{app:record}

\textbf{Source revision.} Reproduced code: upstream MAMEX, \url{https://github.com/L2R-UET/MAMEX}, commit \code{88a932eca821a5657b2b7fc0b6445e4685f4dfab}. This project's repository (URL on page 1) is a package restructure of that code; the model, loss and training loop are unchanged except for the \code{--train_with_vgr} and \code{--train_noise_rate} switches (both default off). The training and evaluation code did not change in behaviour after 17 October 2026: the experiments ran between 16 and 23 October 2026 during development, and later edits touched reporting scripts, file layout and documentation. The exception is the noise-augmented ablation of 28 October, which added \code{--train_noise_rate} and three runs; the other runs were not repeated. The repository history was built on 27 October, after the experiments, so commit timestamps do not show when the runs were made. The tables regenerate identically from the saved outputs.

\textbf{Environment.} Linux 7.0.0; Python 3.13.9; PyTorch 2.9.1 for the core runs as recorded then (2.14.1+cu130 installed on 28 October, used for the ablation, gate-probe and representation-probe runs; 2.9.1 reinstalled in a separate environment on 29 October for the check above); Intel Core i9-12900K, 62~GB RAM; NVIDIA RTX A4000. Package versions are pinned in the repository's requirements file. Setup: create a virtual environment, install the matching \code{torch} wheel, then the requirements.

\textbf{Licence.} Amazon review data are distributed by their authors for research under their own terms; check the original dataset page before redistributing. The upstream README states the MIT licence, but the upstream repository ships no \code{LICENSE} file at the base commit.

\textbf{Seeds and configuration.} Training seeds 2025, 2026, 2027 for all main results (ten baseline seeds, 2025--2034, for diagnostic D1). The corruption seed defaults to the training seed, so one seed gives one corruption mask in every mode; the validation mask for $\gamma$ selection uses the seed plus 10,000. Other settings are the upstream defaults: learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch 2,048, 128-d embeddings, up to 1,000 epochs, early stopping after 200 epochs without improvement, alignment weight 1. The same seed and code give the same clean result (checked on baseline and stress test, seed 2025); GPU nondeterminism was not otherwise audited.

\textbf{Commands} (from the repository root):
\begin{itemize}
\item tests (100, no GPU): \code{PYTHONPATH=src python -m pytest -q}
\item short check on the real data (3 epochs; not a reported result): \url{PYTHONPATH=src python main.py --data_path processed_data_baby/cold --mode baseline --seed 1 --epoch 3 --eva_interval 1 --suffix smoketest}; it writes two \url{smoketest} files into the results folder, which can be deleted
\item core grid: \url{DATA_PATH=processed_data_baby/cold SEEDS="2025 2026 2027" ./scripts/run_experiments.sh} (baseline, stress test and improved mode per seed)
\item single-seed sweeps (noise rate, $\gamma$): \url{PARALLEL=1 SKIP_CORE=1 SWEEPS=1 SEEDS=2025 DATA_PATH=processed_data_baby/cold ./scripts/run_experiments.sh}
\item diagnostics: \url{STREAM=A|B|C DATA_PATH=processed_data_baby/cold ./scripts/run_discrepancy.sh} (A: baseline seeds 2028--2034; B and C: alignment weights 1.28, 12.8 and 128)
\item tables, failure analysis, ranking metrics and intervals, figures: the \code{make_tables}, \code{failure_analysis}, \code{ranking_metrics} and \code{plot_results} modules of the reporting package, run on the saved outputs.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Cost and checkpoints.} One RTX A4000; about 19 minutes of training per run when run alone. The 9 core runs took 2.8 hours of training in total; the 14 single-seed sweep runs bring the total over the first 23 result files to 10.8 hours, and the 16 diagnostic runs (three in parallel, hence 2,000--3,600~s each) add 11.9 hours, for 22.7 hours over 39 result files; the three noise-augmented runs (three in parallel, 2,333--2,554~s each) add 2.0 hours, for 24.7 hours over 42 result files; the six gate-probe runs (three seeds, with and without augmentation, six in parallel, 4,400--5,100~s each) add 8.0 hours, for 32.7 hours over 48 result files; the six representation-probe runs (same design, 5,100--5,900~s each with seven jobs on the GPU) add 9.3 hours, for 42.0 hours over 54 result files; the three checkpoint runs in PyTorch 2.9.1 (2,500--2,700~s each, three in parallel) add 2.2 hours. The 39 repair evaluations of H10 (no training) took about 20 minutes in total. The two H11 checkpoint runs (seeds 2028, 2029; 968 s and 1,656 s, run one after the other) add 0.7 hours, for 2.9 hours of checkpoint training; their two evaluations took about two minutes. No checkpoints are saved or published: the best-epoch weights are kept in memory and used for evaluation, so every result comes from retraining with the seeds above. Because the evaluation-time conditions (noise rate, VGR, $\gamma$, controls) do not change training, runs with the same seed retrain the same clean model (identical clean metrics were observed); saving one checkpoint per seed and evaluating all conditions on it would have cut most of the 32.7 hours. Running the experiments on CPU was not tried. The results that need a GPU are all of the training runs; the tests do not.
